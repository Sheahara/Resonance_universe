# CASCADE - PART TWO: ASSESSMENT
## July 6, 2026 | Falling River Medical Cooperative

---

### VII. THE ARRIVAL

The resonance diagnostics bay had a particular hum to it—a white-noise baseline that most people stopped noticing after a few minutes. Shea Eccari had worked here long enough that she could read the room by its harmonics alone. The way sound bounced off equipment, off walls, off people. The subtle shifts in air pressure when doors opened.

Right now, the bay sounded like controlled panic.

"Incoming," she said to Gideon, and the black shepherd rose smoothly from his down-stay, positioning himself at her left hip. His harness handle settled into her palm with familiar weight.

She heard the ambulance bay doors before they opened—that particular hydraulic wheeze—and then the stretcher wheels, and voices. Aria's voice, clipped and professional. Alexander's, lower, steadier. A male voice she didn't recognize, raw-edged with fear. And underneath all of it, a resonance signature so damaged that Shea stopped walking.

"Doctor Eccari?" One of the nurses. "Are you—"

"I'm fine." She wasn't fine. She was standing in the middle of the corridor trying to make sense of what she was perceiving, because it didn't make sense. Couldn't make sense.

She'd been reading auras for twenty-three years. She'd seen damage before—suppression scarring, field collapse, the particular hollowness that came from EVE depletion. She'd seen people broken by collars and people broken by their own abilities and people broken by things she'd never fully understood.

She had never seen anything like this.

The stretcher passed her. Shea turned to follow, Gideon matching her pace automatically, and she let her perception open fully.

Fissures. Not hairline cracks but *canyons*, running through what should have been a coherent energy field. Dark voids where the suppression had literally eaten through the substrate—suppression voids, she recognized distantly, having read about them but never actually encountered them. The shield layer was gone, not weakened but *absent*, and underneath—

Underneath, Shea could see the architecture of a mind that had built its own last-ditch fortification. A safe room. A bunker. The psychic equivalent of a panic room where someone had retreated with whatever they could carry and locked the door behind them.

One signature was allowed through that barrier. One resonance pattern that the defenses recognized as *safe*.

Shea followed the thread of that pattern to the man walking beside the stretcher. Tall, she thought, based on where his voice came from. Surgeon's hands, probably—there was something precise about the way he moved. He was talking continuously, a low steady stream of words, and the woman on the stretcher was tracking him like he was the only fixed point in a spinning universe.

"Dr. Shahrazai," Shea said, and Aria appeared at her elbow. "Who is he?"

"Collin Blake. Her husband."

"The surgeon?"

"Trauma. Yes."

"He's the only signature she's letting through." Shea kept her voice low. "Whatever she's built in there—whatever's protecting what's left of her—it's keyed to him."

Aria was quiet for a moment. Then: "How bad?"

"Worse than anything I've seen. Worse than I thought was survivable, honestly. The fact that she's still *there* at all—" Shea shook her head. "She's bought herself time by retreating into that safe room, but it's not sustainable. The walls are already showing stress fractures. Hours, maybe. Not days."

"Can we get the collar off?"

"I don't know yet. I need to examine it properly. But Aria—" Shea reached out, found Aria's arm, gripped it. "Even if we get it off cleanly, she doesn't have enough left to stabilize on her own. Her field is in pieces. Without external support, she'll fragment."

"What kind of external support?"

Shea hesitated. This was the part she'd been dreading since she first perceived that safe room and the single signature it allowed through.

"A protective reciprocal binding," she said finally. "A PRB."

Aria went very still.

"I know," Shea said. "I know what I'm asking. I know what it means. But her defenses are already structured around him. The dynamic they have—whatever it is, however it works—her subconscious has decided he's *safe*. That's a foundation I can work with. Without it, I'd be trying to build scaffolding on quicksand."

"She can't consent to that."

"No. She can't."

"Collin doesn't know what he'd be agreeing to."

"No. He doesn't." Shea took a breath. "But you do. And you know what happens if we don't do this."

### VIII. THE CONSULTATION

They put Sciara in Resonance Suite Three—the most shielded room in the building, lined with materials that would dampen any uncontrolled discharge. Collin refused to leave her side. No one tried to make him.

Ava found a chair in the corner and sat down carefully, her left leg aching in that familiar way that meant she'd pushed too hard today. Lyric wasn't here—she'd left the dog with Darren—and she missed the weight of the leash in her hand, the grounding presence. Mira stood beside her, arms crossed, watching everything with the particular intensity of someone who'd finally stopped running from what she knew.

Shea Eccari came in twenty minutes later. Gideon found a spot near the door and settled into a down-stay that said he'd be there as long as necessary.

"I need to explain some things," Shea said without preamble. She was addressing the room, but her face was turned toward Collin's voice. "Some of this is going to sound impossible. Some of it is going to sound like I'm asking you to make an impossible choice. I apologize in advance for both."

Collin didn't look up from Sciara's face. "Tell me."

"Your wife has done something extraordinary. In the face of sustained, catastrophic damage to her resonance field—damage that should have killed her or at minimum destroyed her cognitive function—she constructed a mental defense structure. A safe room, essentially. She took the core of who she is and she *fortified* it."

"That sounds like a good thing."

"It is. It's the only reason she's still here. But it's not sustainable." Shea moved closer to the bed, and Gideon adjusted his position to maintain contact. "The walls of that structure are already showing stress fractures. Without intervention, they'll fail. When they do, everything she's protecting will be exposed to the damage she's been shielding it from."

"How long?"

"Hours. Maybe less."

Collin's hand tightened on Sciara's. She made a small sound—not quite words, not quite a whimper—and turned her face toward him.

"What kind of intervention?" he asked.

Shea was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "There's a procedure called a protective reciprocal binding. A PRB. It's—the simplest analogy is an external fixator for broken bones. A framework that holds the damaged structure in place while it heals."

"What does that mean in practice?"

"It means linking your resonance field to hers. Permanently."

The word hung in the air.

"You'd become, essentially, her external stabilization system," Shea continued. "Your field would reinforce hers. Protect it while it heals. The bond would be—" She paused, choosing her words. "Intimate. Profound. Irreversible."

"You're talking about a lifebond."

"Yes."

Collin was silent. Ava watched his face—watched him process, calculate, weigh. She'd seen that expression before. The surgeon's mind, running through options and outcomes and probabilities.

"Why me?" he asked finally.

"Because her defenses are already built around you." Shea's voice was gentle. "Whatever your relationship is, however it works—her subconscious has decided that your signature is *safe*. You're the only one she's letting through right now. That gives us a foundation. Without it, I'd be trying to build on nothing."

"She can't consent to this."

"No."

"So you're asking me to make a permanent, irreversible decision about her life without her input."

"Yes." Shea didn't flinch. "I'm also asking you to make a permanent, irreversible decision about *your* life, with full knowledge of what it means. I won't pretend this is fair to either of you."

Ava stood up. Her leg protested; she ignored it.

"Collin." She waited until he looked at her. "FRMC has provisions for situations like this. Emergency medical decisions when the patient can't consent. I can act as guardian ad litem—an independent party who reviews the proposed treatment and confirms it's in Sciara's best interest."

"You're not independent. You're her friend."

"I know. But I'm also the person who's been working with her for three months. I understand her values, her priorities, what she would want if she could speak for herself." Ava met his eyes. "And I'm telling you—if there's any chance this saves her, she'd want you to take it. She'd rather be bound to you forever than not be *her* anymore."

Mira spoke for the first time. "She's right, Collin. You know she's right."

Collin looked down at Sciara. At her unfocused eyes, her slack face, the collar still glowing steady red around her throat.

"The collar," he said. "We have to deal with that too."

"Yes." Shea nodded. "And that's complicated. But I think—I think the binding might actually help. Right now, the device is locked at maximum suppression. It's not responding to external overrides. But if we establish the PRB first, your field might be able to... buffer her. Absorb some of the feedback when we attempt removal."

"Might."

"Dr. Blake, I'm not going to lie to you. There are no guarantees here. But without the binding, removal will almost certainly kill her. With it, we have a chance."

Collin closed his eyes. Ava watched him breathe—one breath, two, three—and she knew what he was going to say before he said it.

"Do it," he said. "Whatever you have to do. Just—save her."

### IX. THE TECHNICAL PROBLEM

Alexander found Aria in the equipment room, running diagnostics on the scanner data they'd pulled from Sciara's collar during transport.

"How bad?" he asked.

"Worse than I thought." Aria pulled up a schematic. "Look at this. The housing is standard Series VI. But the firmware signature, the behavioral patterns—this is a VII Adaptive running a protocol I've never seen before."

"Custom work?"

"Has to be." She zoomed in on a data cluster. "The serial number has a .spec suffix. That means Prometheus built this to order."

"For Sevron?"

"For someone. The authorization chain is buried deep, but..." Aria trailed off, staring at the screen. "Alexander. Look at this adaptive learning curve. The device has been *training itself* to interpret her stress responses as resonance use. It's not a bug or a miscalibration. It's doing exactly what it was designed to do."

"Which is?"

"Break her." Aria's voice was flat. "Create a situation where any emotional response triggers suppression. Fear, pain, hope, relief—all of it reads as volitional use, all of it gets punished. She couldn't win no matter what she did."

Alexander was quiet for a moment. Then: "I have contacts at Prometheus. Technical side, not management. People who owe me favors from back when—" He stopped. They didn't talk about 2013 often. "There might be a way to get the master authorization token. Override the lockout."

"And if the device has dual authorization?"

"Then we have half of what we need, and we're no better off than before."

Aria turned to face him. "Shea wants to do a PRB first. Before we attempt removal."

Alexander's expression didn't change, but she felt the shift in him. The way he went still.

"It's the only way," Aria said quietly. "Her field is too damaged. Without external stabilization—"

"I know." He reached out, took her hand. Their bond hummed between them—that constant low-level awareness of each other that had been part of their lives for thirteen years. An accident, in their case. An emergency measure that had become something neither of them would give up.

"It's not the same," Aria said. "What happened to us—that was survival. This is a choice."

"Is it? Sounds like she dies without it."

"Collin has to agree. He has to understand what he's agreeing to."

"Does anyone really understand, going in?" Alexander squeezed her hand. "We didn't."

"No. We didn't." Aria took a breath. "Okay. Assuming Shea can establish the binding, and assuming it provides enough stabilization for Collin's field to buffer her—we still have to deal with the device itself."

"Options?"

"Limited." Aria pulled up another screen. "The collar is running a custom AI that's been trained to interpret anything unusual as a threat. Direct tampering will trigger the failsafe."

"Which does what?"

"Fries whatever's left of her neural pathways." Aria's jaw tightened. "But here's the thing—the failsafe is designed to protect proprietary data as much as anything else. When it triggers, the device will transmit its full operational log back to the home server before it bricks itself. Timestamps, decision trees, the whole history of what it's been doing and why."

Alexander saw where she was going. "If we could capture that transmission—"

"It would be almost as good as a signed confession. Every decision the device made, every time it punished her for being afraid, every escalation—all of it, documented and timestamped."

"So we need to trigger the failsafe in a controlled way. Redirect the energy discharge somewhere that isn't Sciara's nervous system, and capture the data before it reaches Prometheus."

"Essentially."

Alexander considered this. "The energy redirection is the hard part. The discharge is designed to follow the path of least resistance, which is—"

"The neural interface. Yes."

"But if we could create an alternative path. Something more attractive than her nervous system."

"Like what?"

Alexander was quiet, thinking. Then: "The PRB. If Collin's field is actively linked to hers, stabilizing her—his field might be able to absorb the discharge."

Aria stared at him. "You're suggesting we use her husband as a lightning rod."

"I'm suggesting his field might be able to dissipate energy that would destroy hers. It wouldn't be pleasant. But it wouldn't be fatal either." He paused. "Assuming the binding is strong enough."

"That's a lot of assumptions."

"Do we have better options?"

Aria looked at the data on her screen. The collar's operational history, laid out in damning detail. Three months of systematic torture, disguised as medical care.

"No," she said finally. "We don't."

### X. THE PREPARATION

Shea found Ava in the hallway outside Suite Three, leaning against the wall with her eyes closed.

"You should sit down," Shea said. Gideon's tags jingled as he adjusted position.

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. Your gait was uneven when you came in, and you've been on your feet for—" Shea paused, calculating. "At least three hours. Probably longer."

Ava opened her eyes. "How do you do that?"

"Sound. The way weight shifts, the rhythm of footsteps." Shea moved to stand beside her. "Aria told me you're volunteering as guardian ad litem."

"Someone has to."

"It should probably be someone who isn't emotionally involved."

"Find me someone who isn't emotionally involved and also understands what's at stake." Ava's voice was tired. "This isn't a normal situation, Dr. Eccari. There isn't a protocol for 'patient's collar was deliberately modified to torture her into psychic collapse.' I'm doing the best I can with what we have."

Shea was quiet for a moment. Then: "You've been working with her since March."

"Since April. After the initial placement, yes."

"You saw the deterioration."

"I watched it happen and couldn't stop it." Ava's voice cracked, just slightly. "I filed complaints. Collin filed complaints. Her neurologist filed complaints. And Sciara withdrew her consent, and there was nothing—*nothing*—any of us could do after that."

"Why did she withdraw?"

"I don't know. She wouldn't say. Just kept apologizing for wasting everyone's time." Ava pressed her palms against the wall behind her. "Sevron threatened someone. That's the only explanation that makes sense. Mira, maybe. Or me. Or—"

She stopped.

"Or?"

"I have a daughter. Sophie. She's two and a half." Ava's voice had gone flat. Controlled. "Sevron knew about her. Mentioned her once, in a meeting. Just casually. 'How's little Sophie doing? Such a sweet age.'"

Shea felt cold spread through her chest. "You think she threatened your child?"

"I think Sciara *believed* she might. Whether Sevron actually would..." Ava shook her head. "I don't know. I don't know what she's capable of anymore."

"The collar modifications suggest she's capable of quite a lot."

"Yes. They do."

They stood in silence for a moment. Inside Suite Three, Collin was still talking to Sciara. The cadence of his voice carried through the door—low, steady, endless.

"The binding will use their existing dynamic as a foundation," Shea said finally. "You know about that."

"I know." Ava didn't elaborate.

"It's not typical."

"No."

"But it's strong. Established. Her subconscious trusts him at a level that goes beyond ordinary partnership." Shea turned her face toward the door, listening. "Whatever they've built together—it's going to save her life."

"If this works."

"If this works," Shea agreed. "I'm going to start the preparation in about twenty minutes. The binding itself will take several hours. During that time, I need everyone who isn't essential to stay out of the suite."

"I'll make sure Mira knows."

"And I'll need you to formally sign off on the procedure. Guardian ad litem authority."

Ava pushed off the wall. Her leg twinged; she ignored it.

"Show me where to sign," she said.
