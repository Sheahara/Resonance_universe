# SIMULATION: MEDICAL/PERSONAL - CASCADE
## July 6, 2026 | San Rafael, California

---

### I.

The light hurt.

Everything hurt, but the light was the worst—slicing through Sciara's closed eyelids like broken glass, fragmenting into colors that didn't belong to any spectrum she could name. She kept her eyes shut and tried to remember what day it was. What month. What year.

The collar hummed against her throat.

*CFL 4. Volitional resonance detected. Initiating corrective feedback.*

She wasn't doing anything. She was lying in bed, trying not to exist too loudly, and the collar thought she was—

The feedback hit like a nail driven through her temple. Her whole body seized, fingers clawing at the sheets, and somewhere far away she heard herself make a sound that wasn't quite a scream.

"Sciara. *Sciara.*"

Collin's voice, cutting through the static. His hand found hers—warm, solid, real—and she latched onto it like a drowning woman grabbing a rope.

"I'm here," he said. "I'm right here. Try to breathe."

She tried. The air came in wrong, hitching and stuttering, and the collar interpreted that as volitional resonance too because apparently existing while afraid was a containment violation now.

*CFL 5. Escalating corrective protocol.*

"No," she whispered. "No, please, I'm not—I'm not *doing* anything—"

Collin's other hand came up to cup her face, anchoring her. "Look at me. Just look at me."

She forced her eyes open. The light stabbed through her skull, but Collin was there—his face swimming in and out of focus, jaw tight with the controlled expression she knew meant he was terrified.

"There you are," he said, softer. "Stay with me."

The collar eased back to CFL 3. Still painful. Still wrong. But survivable.

Sciara tried to nod and immediately regretted it. The room tilted violently, walls sliding sideways, and she barely managed to turn her head before the nausea hit.

Collin had the basin ready. Of course he did. He'd been ready for this for hours now—the basin, the wet cloth, the glass of water she couldn't keep down. She'd lost track of how many times they'd been through this cycle.

When it passed, she lay back against the pillows, trembling. The headache was a living thing now, pulsing behind her eyes, spreading tendrils down her neck and into her spine. The lupus flare made everything worse—joints swollen and screaming, skin hypersensitive, the butterfly rash across her cheeks hot to the touch.

"I'm calling Ava," Collin said.

Sciara's hand shot out, grabbing his wrist. "No. Collin, you can't—"

"I can, and I am." His voice had that particular quality—quiet, immovable. Not a request. "Your shield integrity is at four percent. *Four*. You're having seizures. You can't keep fluids down. This is beyond what I can manage at home."

"If Dr. Sevron finds out—"

"I don't care what Sevron does." There was something dark in his voice now, something that made Sciara's chest tighten. "I'm not taking you back there. Do you understand? I will take you to a civilian hospital before I take you back to the ICR."

The collar pulsed. *CFL 4.*

Sciara closed her eyes against the feedback, breathing through it the way Ava had taught her. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six. The numbers helped. Something to focus on besides the pain.

"She'll hurt them," she managed. "She said—"

"I know what she said." Collin's thumb traced circles on the inside of her wrist, finding her pulse point. The touch was deliberate, grounding. "But you're dying, Sciara. Right now, in front of me, you are dying. And I won't let that happen. Whatever the cost."

She wanted to argue. She wanted to explain that he didn't understand—that Talia Sevron was patient and methodical and utterly without conscience, that she'd already proven she could get to anyone she wanted. Mira. Ava. Sophie, who was eleven years old and had nothing to do with any of this.

But the words kept slipping away, fragmenting before she could form them. The world was going grey at the edges, and she couldn't remember if she'd already said this or only thought it.

"Okay," she whispered. The word felt like surrender. "Okay."

Collin leaned down and pressed his lips to her forehead, just above the rash. "Good girl. I've got you."

The collar didn't spike at that. The collar never seemed to notice when Collin's words slid through her defenses, finding the place inside her that needed to be held. Maybe because there was no resonance in it—just trust. Just the quiet architecture of something they'd built together, one small piece at a time.

Sciara let herself drift, listening to him move around the room. The phone. His voice, low and urgent.

"Ava, it's Collin. I need your help."

### II.

Ava Chen was elbow-deep in soapy water when her phone rang.

The kitchen was chaos—the comfortable, lived-in kind that came from having her whole family under one roof on a Sunday afternoon. Her mother was teaching Sophie how to fold dumplings at the counter, both of them dusted with flour. Her father was pretending to read the paper while actually watching sports highlights on his tablet with the volume low. Mira was cross-legged on the couch in the living room, ostensibly reviewing case files but mostly providing color commentary on her grandmother's cooking technique.

"Your filling is too wet," Mrs. Chen was saying in Cantonese. "You see? It will break through the wrapper."

"*Poh Poh*," Sophie protested, "you literally just told me to add more liquid."

"A little more. Not swimming pool."

Mira caught Ava's eye and grinned, and Ava felt something settle in her chest—that particular warmth that came from having the people she loved in one place, safe and whole.

Then her phone rang, and the number on the screen made the warmth evaporate.

She dried her hands and stepped onto the back porch, sliding the door closed behind her. The fog was rolling in off the Bay, softening the edges of the afternoon.

"Collin. What's wrong?"

*"It's Sciara."* His voice was steady, but Ava had known Collin long enough to hear what lived underneath that steadiness. *"She's—it's bad, Ava. Shield integrity is under five percent. She's been having what I think are seizures—she wasn't convulsing but she was unresponsive and when she came back she didn't know where she was. The headaches won't stop, the vertigo's constant, and the lupus flare we talked about last week has completely—"*

"Slow down." Ava kept her voice calm, professional. "When did the seizures start?"

*"I'm not sure. I woke up at four this morning and she was already—she was just lying there staring at nothing. I couldn't get her to respond for almost ten minutes."*

Post-ictal. Ava felt her stomach drop. That meant he'd missed the actual seizure—or seizures, plural. No way to know how many or how long.

*"I'm not taking her to the ICR,"* Collin continued. *"I can't. If you can't help, I'll take her to ZSFG or Stanford, I'll make something up, but I am not bringing her back to Sevron."*

"You won't have to." Ava was already calculating logistics, running through protocols. "I have the authority to authorize an inter-agency transfer if it's medically indicated, and this is clearly medically indicated. Even if I didn't—you're her proxy. You requested transfer of care. I'd have to be pretty creative to justify refusing that."

*"Can Sevron block it?"*

"She can try." Ava's jaw tightened. "She won't succeed. Not this time. Collin, I'm going to call Falling River and have them send a private ambulance. It'll avoid the emergency channels that ICR monitors. Can you text me your address?"

*"Already sent."*

Of course he had. Collin was meticulous like that—already three steps ahead while appearing perfectly composed.

*"Ava."* A pause. *"She's scared. She keeps talking about... she's worried about what Sevron will do if she goes somewhere else. Who she might go after."*

Ava closed her eyes. She'd suspected something like this ever since Sciara had withdrawn her ethics complaint—that sudden, inexplicable reversal that had left everyone baffled. Sciara wasn't someone who gave up easily. Which meant someone had given her a reason to.

"I'm going to bring Mira with me," she said. "We'll be there as soon as we can. The ambulance should arrive around the same time. Just—keep her calm. Keep her talking if you can. And Collin? Don't let her look at the user app again. Whatever that number says, it's not going to help her right now."

*"Understood."*

The line went dead.

Ava stood on the porch for a moment, watching the fog erase the hills in the distance. Then she went back inside.

Mira looked up the moment the door opened, already reading Ava's expression. The case file she'd been reviewing was forgotten, her body language shifting from relaxed to alert in the span of a breath.

"What happened?"

"Sciara. It's bad." Ava kept her voice low, mindful of Sophie and her parents. "I need to go. And I think you should come with me."

Something flickered across Mira's face—the particular tension that always appeared when Sciara's case came up. They'd talked about this, late at night when neither of them could sleep. How Sciara's collar behaved in ways that didn't match the specs. How Sevron had been systematically isolating her from her support system. How the ethics complaints had disappeared without explanation.

"Give me thirty seconds."

Mira was already moving, gathering her bag, pulling on her jacket. Ava crossed to the kitchen, where her mother was watching with the sharp-eyed awareness that came from decades of reading between lines.

"I have to go. Work emergency."

Her mother nodded, wiping flour-dusted hands on her apron. "The girl from your caseload? The one who was here for dinner last month?"

Ava had forgotten about that—she'd invited Sciara and Collin over during one of her better weeks, back in April when things had seemed almost manageable. Sophie had been fascinated by Sciara's work at the archives, peppering her with questions about historical documents until Collin had gently redirected the conversation.

"Yes."

"Go," her mother said. "We'll save you dumplings."

Sophie looked up from her (admittedly soggy) dumpling wrapper. "Is she okay? Is she—"

"She's going to be fine." Ava made herself smile. "I just need to help her get to the right doctors. I'll be back for dinner, okay?"

She wouldn't be, probably. But Sophie didn't need to carry that.

On the way to the car, Mira fell into step beside her. "I'm going to ask you something, and I need you to answer honestly."

Ava clicked the remote to unlock the doors. "Okay."

"You're not just going as her BSP, are you. This is something else."

Ava paused with her hand on the driver's door, considering how much to say. Mira deserved the truth—but the truth would make her a target, if she wasn't already.

Then again, she was already involved. Had been since March, when she'd been the one to call for medical after the containment breach that had started everything. She'd seen Sevron in that room, standing over Sciara's unconscious body with the collar already in place.

"Falling River isn't just a hospital," Ava said quietly. "It's... there are people there who specialize in cases like Sciara's. Cases where the collar itself might be part of the problem."

Mira's expression didn't change, but something shifted behind her eyes. "I always wondered. You and the collar removal program. The way you talked about it."

"I'm sorry I couldn't tell you more. I wanted to give you—"

"Plausible deniability." Mira's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "I figured. And I appreciate the thought, Ava, but I've been in the SAFE program for three years. I've watched what it does to people. If there's somewhere that can actually help..." She shook her head. "I want to be part of that. Whatever it costs."

Ava looked at her—really looked. Mira Fallon, twenty-six years old, collared since she was twenty-three. The scars on her wrists that she thought nobody noticed. The particular way she flinched whenever a CFL notification came through. The fact that she was still standing, still fighting, still trying to be good despite everything the program had put her through.

"Okay," Ava said. "Get in. I'll explain on the way."

### III.

The ambulance was already pulling up when Ava's car reached the address in San Rafael—a modest craftsman bungalow on a quiet street, fog-wrapped and still. The vehicle was unmarked white, deliberately anonymous, but Ava recognized the two figures emerging from the back.

Aria Shahrazai moved with the careful grace of late pregnancy, one hand resting on the curve of her belly as she stepped down from the ambulance. She was nearly seven months along now with twins, and FRMC's medical director had tried to insist she stay on bed rest—a battle Aria had apparently won through sheer force of personality. Alexander followed close behind, his hand hovering at the small of her back in a gesture that was equal parts protective and practiced.

Mira's breath caught audibly. "That's—is that—"

"Later," Ava said. "I promise. Later."

She parked and crossed the lawn at something just short of a jog, Mira keeping pace beside her. Aria met them at the door, her dark eyes sharp with professional focus despite the physical toll the pregnancy was obviously taking.

"Ava. I got your message." Aria's voice was calm, measured. "We brought full diagnostic kit and emergency stabilization supplies. What's the current status?"

"Shield integrity under five percent as of an hour ago. Possible non-convulsive seizures. Refractory headache, severe vertigo, lupus flare that's been escalating for weeks. And the collar—"

"Is behaving abnormally. Yes." Aria's jaw tightened. "The specs in her file say series VI standard configuration. I want to see that for myself."

The door opened before Ava could knock. Collin filled the doorway—tall, dark-haired, with the kind of contained stillness that Ava had learned to read as carefully managed fear.

"Thank you for coming." His voice was steady, but his eyes went immediately to Aria, assessing. "Dr. Shahrazai. I didn't expect—"

"I don't send other people to do what I can do myself." Aria stepped past him into the house, Alexander and the ambulance crew following with their equipment. "Where is she?"

"Bedroom. Down the hall, last door on the left. She's—" Collin paused, something cracking beneath the composure. "She's conscious, but she's not processing well. She might not understand what's happening."

Aria nodded, already moving. Collin fell into step beside her, and Ava watched him reorient—the subtle shift from fear to focus, the way he seemed to pull himself together by force of will.

Mira hung back, and when Ava glanced at her, she saw why.

Mira's face had gone pale. She was staring at nothing, her hands trembling at her sides, and Ava recognized the particular quality of her stillness. She was Seeing—the capital-S kind, the unshielded awareness that came from being a Type II Empath with her own damaged shields.

"Mira?"

"I can see it." Mira's voice came out hoarse. "Her aura. I can see it from *here*, Ava. I shouldn't be able to do that. She's behind walls, she's—I shouldn't be able to—"

"What do you see?"

Mira's hands came up, pressing against her own temples. "Fissures. Everywhere. Like cracks in glass, but *glowing*. And there are these—these voids. Places where there's just... nothing. Like something ate holes in her."

Suppression voids. Ava had read about them in the FRMC literature—rare, catastrophic damage caused by overuse of suppression technology. The collar literally burning out sections of neural tissue, leaving gaps in the psyche that couldn't be repaired.

"Ava." Mira lowered her hands, and her eyes were wet. "What did they do to her?"

Before Ava could answer, a sound came from down the hall—not a scream, but something worse. A low, keening cry that cut off abruptly, replaced by the mechanical chime of collar feedback.

They ran.

### IV.

Sciara didn't understand.

There were people in her room. Too many people. Voices overlapping, hands reaching for her, and the collar was screaming *threat threat threat* while her body tried to crawl backward through the headboard.

*CFL 6. Critical resonance event detected. Initiating emergency suppression.*

"No," she gasped. "No, please, I'm not—I don't—"

"Sciara." Collin's voice cut through the static. "Sciara, look at me. These people are here to help. You're going to Falling River, remember? Not the ICR. Falling River."

Falling River. The name snagged on something, but she couldn't hold onto it. The collar was drowning everything, and the woman standing over her had dark eyes and dark hair and she looked like—she looked like—

"I'm not Sevron," the woman said, her voice gentle but firm. "My name is Aria. I'm a doctor. I'm going to help you, but I need you to try to stay calm."

*CFL 7. Volitional resistance detected.*

It wasn't resistance. It wasn't anything except terror and confusion, but the collar didn't know the difference. Couldn't know the difference. Every spike of adrenaline registered as defiance, every instinctive flinch as attempted escape.

Sciara had figured this out weeks ago. Had tried to explain it to Dr. Sevron during one of their sessions, her voice cracking as she described the impossible trap—how the collar punished her for being afraid, which made her more afraid, which made it punish her more.

Dr. Sevron had smiled and made a note on her tablet. *Adjustment documented. Continue monitoring.*

"Collin." Sciara found his hand, or maybe he found hers. "Collin, I can't—the collar won't—"

"I know." He was crouched beside the bed now, his face level with hers. "I know it doesn't make sense. But these people are different. They're going to figure out what's wrong. All you have to do is let them."

"Can you do that?" Aria again, her voice steady. "Can you try to let us help you?"

Sciara wanted to say yes. She wanted to be the person who could do that—trust strangers, accept help, believe that things might get better. But the last time she'd tried to accept help, Talia Sevron had shown her surveillance footage of Mira walking alone to her car after a late shift. Had listed Ava's home address, Sophie's school schedule, the name of the coffee shop where Collin went every morning.

*If you tell anyone,* Sevron had said, her voice perfectly pleasant, *I will destroy them. One by one. And I will make sure you know it was your fault.*

"I can't," Sciara whispered. "I can't, I'm sorry, I can't—"

The collar hit CFL 8.

She'd never felt it go that high before. The pain was beyond pain—a white-hot erasure that wiped out thought, identity, everything except the animal imperative to *make it stop*.

Distantly, she heard shouting. Felt hands on her, holding her down. Something pressed against her neck, and then—

Silence.

Not the silence of unconsciousness, but something else. A dampening. The collar was still there, still humming against her throat, but its voice had gone quiet, muffled beneath something that felt like fog.

"Got it." A man's voice, tight with concentration. "Emergency resonance suppression field. It'll buy us maybe twenty minutes before the collar adapts."

"Then we move now." Aria, sharp and commanding. "Alexander, get the stretcher. Ava, I need you with me in the ambulance—she's going to need a familiar face when she comes back up. Mira..."

"I'll help." Mira's voice, shaking but determined. "Whatever you need. I'll help."

Sciara felt herself being lifted. The world tilted and swam, but the pain stayed muffled, and Collin's hand stayed locked around hers.

"I'm coming with you," he said. Not a question.

"Of course." Aria's tone softened almost imperceptibly. "Family rides in the ambulance."

Family. The word settled into Sciara's chest like a physical weight. She wasn't Collin's family—wasn't anyone's family, not really. Just a problem to be solved, a risk to be managed, a containment breach waiting to happen.

But Collin's hand didn't let go. And when they loaded her into the ambulance, he climbed in after her, taking a seat beside the stretcher like it was the most natural thing in the world.

"You're safe now," he said quietly. "Whatever happens next, you're safe."

She wanted to believe him. She wanted it so badly that something cracked open in her chest, and for a moment the collar almost seemed irrelevant.

Then the ambulance started moving, and the gentle vibration of the engine was enough to set off another wave of vertigo. Sciara closed her eyes and focused on the pressure of Collin's hand, counting seconds between each pulse of her headache.

In for four. Hold for four. Out for six.

The numbers helped. They always helped.

### V.

Aria Shahrazai had seen a lot of collars in her time—her own, once, and countless others since she'd dedicated her career to undoing the damage they caused. She'd seen standard VI configurations and experimental modifications, collars that had been calibrated with care and collars that had been weaponized through neglect.

She'd never seen anything quite like this.

"The external housing is series VI standard," she said, studying the readout on her portable scanner. "But the behavioral signature isn't even close. Look at this response latency."

Ava leaned over to examine the data. "That's... that shouldn't be possible. Standard VI has a minimum threshold before it registers activity. This is interpreting baseline autonomic function as volitional resonance."

"Because it's not a standard VI." Aria's voice was grim. "The behavioral signature matches a series VII prototype. Adaptive learning protocols, predictive suppression, the works. Someone took VII guts and stuffed them in a VI shell."

"That's—" Ava stopped, shaking her head. "Why would anyone do that?"

"To avoid accountability. VII series requires additional authorization, additional oversight. It's logged differently in the system. But if it's *housed* in VI hardware and *logged* as VI..." Aria didn't finish the sentence. She didn't need to.

The ambulance hit a bump, and Sciara whimpered—a small, broken sound that made something tighten in Aria's chest. She remembered making that sound herself, once. Fifteen years ago and half a lifetime away, but some things you didn't forget.

"The adaptive protocols have gone completely off the rails," Aria continued, keeping her voice clinical. It helped. "The collar is learning, but it's learning the wrong things. It's interpreting any stress response as defiance and calibrating its suppression accordingly."

"So she's in a feedback loop."

"A feedback loop designed to be unwinnable." Aria met Ava's eyes. "This wasn't a miscalibration, Ava. This was deliberate. Someone programmed this collar to induce learned helplessness. To break her."

The words hung in the air between them, heavy and horrible.

Collin's voice came from the other side of the stretcher, low and controlled. "Can you remove it?"

"Not yet." Aria hated saying it. "The collar is too integrated with her system right now. If we try to remove it without proper preparation, the shock could finish what the device started. We need to stabilize her first, get a full picture of what we're dealing with. Resonance diagnostics, comprehensive neurological workup, expanded tox screens. I'm not assuming any of her ICR records are accurate."

"They're not." Sciara's voice was barely a whisper, but it made everyone in the ambulance go still. Her eyes were open, glassy with pain but present in a way they hadn't been before. "Nothing... nothing Sevron documented is real. She made me... she watched while I..."

"Shh." Collin squeezed her hand. "You don't have to explain right now."

"I do." There was something desperate in her voice now, the words tumbling out in fragments. "The tests. The sessions. She'd make the collar spike and then ask me to describe what I was feeling. She said it was... it was data collection. For my treatment plan. But it wasn't. It was..."

She couldn't finish. The collar, even dampened by Alexander's emergency field, gave a warning pulse—CFL climbing in response to her distress.

"Okay." Aria leaned forward, letting Sciara see her face clearly. "I hear you. And when you're ready, when you're stable enough, we're going to document everything. Every session, every test, everything she did to you. But right now, your only job is to survive long enough to tell that story. Can you do that for me?"

Sciara's eyes found Collin's face, and something passed between them—some silent communication that Aria recognized but couldn't quite name.

"Yes," Sciara whispered. "I can do that."

"Good." Aria sat back. "We'll be at Falling River in about fifteen minutes. Once we're there, we're going to get you out of this trap. I promise."

It was a promise Aria had no business making—there were too many unknowns, too many variables, too many ways this could go wrong. But she'd learned a long time ago that sometimes people needed to hear the promise more than they needed statistical probabilities.

The ambulance drove on through the fog, carrying them toward whatever came next.

### VI.

Mira had never been inside Falling River Medical Center before.

She'd heard of it, of course—whispered rumors in the SAFE participant networks, half-believed stories about a hospital that actually treated resonance damage instead of just managing it. She'd assumed it was a myth, or at best a gross exaggeration.

It wasn't.

The building rose out of the fog like something from a dream, all clean lines and warm wood and floor-to-ceiling windows that caught the diffused afternoon light. Nothing like the ICR facilities she was used to—no harsh fluorescents, no institutional grey, no security checkpoints bristling with suppression tech.

A medical team was waiting at the entrance. They moved with practiced efficiency, transferring Sciara from the ambulance stretcher to a hospital gurney while someone rattled off vitals and another person scanned her collar with equipment Mira had never seen before.

"Shield integrity three percent and dropping," one of the techs reported. "Recommend immediate resonance stabilization protocol."

"Suppression voids confirmed on preliminary scan," another added. "At least four major and several minor. Concentrated in the left temporal region."

Aria was issuing orders, coordinating the team with the calm authority of someone who'd done this a thousand times. "Get her to resonance diagnostics first. I want a complete picture before we do anything else. And page Dr. Okonkwo—tell her it's a VII prototype in VI housing and we need her expertise on the adaptive protocols."

The gurney started moving, and Collin moved with it—staying close to Sciara's side, their hands still linked. Nobody tried to stop him.

Mira stood in the entrance, frozen. She could still see it—Sciara's aura, shattered and gaping, the suppression voids like patches of absolute darkness against the flickering remnants of her field. It was the worst thing she'd ever witnessed. Worse than her own collar activation, worse than the night she'd tried to make it all stop.

"Mira." Ava's hand touched her elbow. "You okay?"

"No." There was no point lying. "Ava, I can see—I can still see it. The damage. The voids. I shouldn't be able to see any of it, not through walls, not without trying. My shields aren't that good."

"Your shields are better than you think." Ava's voice was gentle. "But you're right—what you're seeing isn't normal. Her shields have failed so completely that she's essentially broadcasting. Any Type II with intact perception would be able to read her from across the building right now."

Broadcasting. Mira had read about that—the technical term for when a person's psionic shields collapsed so thoroughly that their emotional state became impossible to filter out. It was supposed to be rare. Temporary. Recoverable.

Looking at the voids in Sciara's aura, Mira wasn't sure any of those things were true.

"We should go in," Ava said. "She's going to need—"

"Wait." Mira grabbed Ava's arm. "There's something I have to tell you first."

Ava went still. "Okay."

"I was there. In March, when the containment breach happened." The words came out in a rush, tumbling over each other. "I was the one who called for medical. When I got there, Sevron was already in the room. Sciara was unconscious, and the collar was already on her neck. It had only been like ten minutes since the breach alarm went off. There's no way she could have gone through proper authorization protocols in that time. I thought—I thought it was weird, but Sevron said it was an emergency measure, that Sciara's resonance had spiked so high they needed immediate containment, and I just—I believed her. Or I told myself I believed her because the alternative was..."

She trailed off, unable to finish.

Ava's expression had gone very still. "You're saying Sevron placed the collar herself. Without proper authorization. Without going through channels."

"Yes."

"And you've known this since March."

"I—" Mira's voice cracked. "I'm sorry. I should have said something. I should have—but I didn't have proof, and Sevron is—you don't understand what she's like, Ava. The way she looks at people. Like she's calculating exactly how to take them apart."

"I understand better than you think." Ava's voice was quiet, but there was something hard beneath it. "Mira, I need you to listen to me very carefully. What you just told me is important. It's evidence. And when Sciara is stable, when we have the documentation we need, it's going to be part of how we bring Sevron down."

"You think you can actually—"

"I think Talia Sevron has been operating with impunity for too long because she's convinced everyone that challenging her is impossible." Ava's jaw tightened. "She's wrong. Now come on. We have work to do."

They walked into Falling River together.

### VII.

The resonance diagnostics suite was unlike anything Collin had ever seen.

He'd been in ICR medical facilities before—cold, clinical spaces where the equipment hummed with barely concealed menace and every surface seemed designed to remind you of your own vulnerability. This was different. The walls were warm wood, the lighting soft and adjustable. The diagnostic equipment itself was sleek and sophisticated, but it had been integrated into the space rather than dominating it.

And the people. God, the people were different.

"I'm going to need you to release her hand for a few minutes," one of the technicians said to him—a young woman with kind eyes and a gentle voice. "The initial scan requires her to be magnetically isolated. But you can stay in the room. You can talk to her the whole time."

"Will she hear me?"

"She'll hear you." The technician smiled. "And honestly? That might help more than anything we do in the next few minutes. Having an anchor—a person, I mean, someone she trusts—that's one of the most stabilizing things for a patient in resonance crisis."

An anchor. Collin looked at Sciara, pale and small on the diagnostic bed, her eyes closed but her fingers still twitching toward where his hand had been.

"Okay," he said. "I understand."

He stepped back to the observation area, positioning himself where Sciara would be able to see him if she opened her eyes. Through the window, he watched as the technicians calibrated their equipment, speaking to each other in low voices full of technical terms he didn't fully understand.

"The collar is definitely VII architecture." That was Aria's voice, coming through the intercom. She'd stationed herself in the control room, coordinating with the diagnostic team despite the obvious exhaustion in her face. "The adaptive learning matrix has been running unchecked for—Christ, has she really had this thing for three months?"

"Collar placement logged March 31st," someone confirmed.

"Three months of maladaptive learning. No wonder the feedback loops are this severe." Aria's voice tightened. "Start the resonance mapping sequence. I want to see exactly where the damage is concentrated."

The diagnostic bed began to hum—a low, subtle vibration that Collin could feel through the floor. On the display screens, images began to appear: complex three-dimensional renderings of what he assumed was Sciara's brain activity, overlaid with swirling patterns of light and shadow.

"Oh, *hell*," one of the technicians breathed.

The images showed it clearly: vast, dark patches where the light should have been. Gaps in the pattern, like holes eaten through fabric. And threading through everything, like cracks in ice, lines of sickly luminescence that pulsed and flickered in irregular rhythms.

"Suppression voids," Aria said grimly. "Multiple. Concentrated in the regions governing emotional regulation and resonance expression. And those fissure patterns—she's been trying to use her abilities through the suppression. Repeatedly. Probably involuntarily."

"The collar would have punished her every time," someone added quietly.

"Yes. And then punished her for the stress response to the punishment. And then punished her for trying not to be stressed." Aria's voice was flat, professional, but Collin could hear the anger beneath it. "This is what happens when you program adaptive suppression technology to interpret all emotional response as defiance."

Sciara's eyes opened.

Collin stepped forward immediately, pressing his palm against the glass. "I'm here. I'm right here."

She turned her head toward his voice, and even through the window, he could see the confusion in her face. The fear. The desperate, searching need to understand what was happening.

"You're at Falling River," he said, keeping his voice steady. "The doctors are running diagnostic scans. They're trying to figure out how to help you. All you have to do is stay still for a few more minutes. Can you do that?"

Her mouth moved, forming words he couldn't hear through the glass. But he could read them well enough: *I'm scared.*

"I know." He kept his hand pressed against the window, wishing desperately that he could touch her. "I know you're scared. But I'm not going anywhere. Whatever happens next, we face it together."

Something shifted in her expression—a tiny loosening of the terror, replaced by something that looked almost like trust.

The collar pulsed.

*CFL 5. Elevated emotional response detected.*

"Goddammit," Aria muttered. "Alexander, can you extend the suppression field? It's starting to adapt."

"I'm trying." Alexander's voice, strained with effort. "The adaptive protocols are fighting me. It's like it's—it's learning how to counter the field in real time."

"That's exactly what it's doing." Aria's fingers flew across the console. "It was designed to overcome resistance. Any resistance. Including ours."

The collar climbed to CFL 6.

Sciara's body arched on the diagnostic bed, a silent scream tearing from her throat. The monitors erupted in alarms, data cascading across the screens faster than anyone could process.

"Abort the scan," Aria ordered. "Get that field stabilized. And for God's sake, someone get me a direct interface with that collar—I need to override the adaptive protocols before they kill her."

Collin stood frozen at the window, watching helplessly as the medical team swarmed around Sciara's bed. He couldn't hear her through the glass, but he could see her face—the agony, the confusion, the desperate animal terror of someone trapped in a nightmare they couldn't escape.

*Whatever happens next, we face it together.*

He'd said that. He'd meant it.

But standing here, watching the woman he loved being slowly destroyed by a piece of technology designed to break her, Collin realized that facing it together might not be enough.

They needed to stop it.

They needed to end this.

And somewhere, in the deepest part of himself—the part that calculated risks and measured probabilities and made decisions other people couldn't—Collin began to plan.

### VIII.

Three hours later, Sciara was stable.

Not healed. Not safe. Just... stable. The collar had been temporarily overridden through a combination of FRMC's resonance suppression technology and Dr. Okonkwo's expertise with adaptive systems. It would buy them time—maybe twelve hours, maybe less—before the collar figured out how to circumvent the override.

Twelve hours to decide what came next.

Collin sat in the chair beside Sciara's bed, watching her sleep. The medication they'd given her had finally taken effect, dragging her down into something deeper than the fitful unconsciousness she'd been drifting through all day. Her face was slack, peaceful in a way it hadn't been for weeks.

She looked young. Vulnerable. Nothing like the fiercely competent archivist he'd fallen for two years ago, the woman who could spend hours explaining the provenance of a medieval manuscript and make it sound like the most fascinating thing in the world.

Talia Sevron had taken that from her. Systematically. Deliberately. Over the course of three months, she'd stripped away everything that made Sciara *Sciara*, leaving behind only fear and pain and the learned certainty that there was no escape.

"She's going to need extensive deprogramming." Aria's voice came from the doorway, quiet enough not to disturb the patient. "The psychological conditioning alone would take months to address. And the physical damage—the suppression voids—some of that may be permanent."

Collin didn't look up. "What are the odds?"

"Of full recovery?" Aria stepped into the room, lowering herself carefully into the other chair. "Honestly? I don't know. We've never seen a case quite like this. The VII prototype was designed for Type VIII containment—reality-benders, narrative manipulators, the most dangerous resonance users in existence. Using it on someone like Sciara—a Type II with moderate abilities—it's like using a sledgehammer to hang a picture frame."

"She's not Type II."

Aria went very still. "What?"

"She's a hybrid." Collin finally looked up, meeting Aria's eyes. "Type II and Type IV, with some Type I expression under stress. Sevron knows. It's why she targeted her."

"That's..." Aria shook her head slowly. "That's not in any of her records."

"Because Sevron falsified them. All of it. The initial assessment, the containment classification, the collar specifications—none of it is real. Sciara figured that out about six weeks ago. She started documenting discrepancies, building a case. She was going to report it."

"And then she withdrew her ethics complaint."

"Because Sevron threatened her." Collin's voice went flat. "Not directly—she's too smart for that. She showed Sciara surveillance footage. Mira walking to her car alone. Ava's home address. The name of Ava's niece's school. She made it very clear what would happen if Sciara didn't back down."

"Jesus Christ."

"Sciara thinks if she asks for help—any help, from anyone—Sevron will follow through. That's why she wouldn't go to another hospital. Why she wouldn't talk to anyone about what was happening. She's been trying to protect everyone by sacrificing herself."

Aria was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice was carefully controlled. "And you're telling me this now because...?"

"Because it's time." Collin looked at Sciara's sleeping face, memorizing the curve of her cheek, the flutter of her pulse at her throat. "Whatever comes next, it has to end. I won't let her destroy herself trying to protect people who would rather fight beside her."

"Collin." Aria leaned forward. "If what you're saying is true—and I believe you—then Sevron is more dangerous than I thought. She has resources, influence, institutional protection. Taking her down isn't going to be simple."

"I know."

"She'll come after you. After everyone who helped today."

"I know that too."

"And you're still—"

"Dr. Shahrazai." Collin's voice cut through her words with quiet precision. "Talia Sevron has spent three months torturing the woman I love. She has used fear as a weapon, isolation as a strategy, and institutional power as a shield. She has counted on our decency, our caution, our willingness to play by rules she has no intention of following."

He stood, crossing to the window. The fog had lifted slightly, revealing a sliver of evening sky.

"I'm done playing by those rules. Whatever it takes to stop her—whatever it costs—I'm in. Are you?"

Aria was quiet for a long time. Then she stood, one hand braced against the small of her back, and moved to stand beside him at the window.

"Yes," she said. "I'm in. And so is Alexander. And Ava. And everyone at this hospital who's watched the ICR destroy people for the crime of being different." Her voice hardened. "Sevron thinks she's untouchable. She's wrong."

"How do we start?"

"First, we stabilize Sciara. Get her strong enough to tell her own story." Aria turned to face him. "Then we document everything. Every discrepancy in her records, every falsified report, every piece of evidence that proves what Sevron did. And when we have enough—"

"We tear it all down."

Aria smiled—a sharp, fierce expression that reminded Collin of the stories he'd heard about her. The containment breach fifteen years ago. The collar burnout that had nearly killed her. The year she'd spent building FRMC's deprogramming protocols from the ground up.

"Yes," she said. "We tear it all down."

Outside the window, the fog continued to lift, revealing the first stars of evening.

Somewhere across the Bay, Talia Sevron was probably congratulating herself on another successful manipulation, another patient broken, another threat contained.

She had no idea what was coming.
